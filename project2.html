01   Introduction

RF Communications in a Contested Theater

The South China Sea is one of the world's most contested waterways, where regional state actors use "salami-slicing" and "cabbage tactics" to assert dominance within the electromagnetic spectrum rather than through direct kinetic conflict. Any UAV RF communication system operating in this environment must account for RF interference, tactical datalink interception, and a baseline link budget robust enough to withstand environmental degradation while guaranteeing a sufficient fade margin.

Because standard ITU databases do not always reflect the reality of a contested grey zone, the design also leans on Open-Source Intelligence (OSINT) and geopolitical risk monitoring ahead of deployment. The system's intended L-Band radio operates at 1060.5 MHz — a frequency sensitive to the tropical maritime boundary layer of the South China Sea — so the link budget dynamically accounts for two environmental constraints scoped for this project:

  • Rain & Moisture Absorption — heavy tropical downpours and high absolute humidity cause localized signal attenuation and hydrometeor scattering.
  • Ionospheric Turbulence — solar activity and ionospheric scintillation can induce rapid phase and amplitude fluctuations that degrade signal coherence.

Frequency validation cross-referenced 1060.5 MHz against the ITU Region 3 allocation table, confirming the band is reserved for Aeronautical Radionavigation Service (ARNS) — a low civilian-interference profile on paper. In practice, the Nine-Dash Line dispute means regional actors reject international spectrum zoning across roughly 90% of the theater, so physical spectrum usage is governed by de facto military enforcement rather than de jure law. Operating at 1060.5 MHz also sits close to the 1030/1090 MHz IFF and Secondary Surveillance Radar bands used by the PLAN, US Navy, and regional coastal forces — so the system must be rigorously filtered for spectral containment to avoid bleeding into those guard bands.

02   Mission Profile & Navigation Plan

SMEM UAV Operating from an LCS-2 Ship

Line-of-Sight (LOS) communications serve as the primary link during launch, climb-out, descent, and recovery, and remain an active redundant backup whenever the aircraft is within physical range of a suitably equipped ship. For long-range operations, Beyond Line-of-Sight (BLOS) communication is the primary command-and-control link through transit and mission loiter. Because the LCS-2 has a wide trimaran flight deck rather than a longitudinal runway, a VTOL configuration was selected for the navigation plan.

03   Link Budget Design (RF LOS)

Signal Link Margin

Net signal margin is the safety buffer between the actual received signal and the minimum power the receiver needs to hold a reliable connection:

Net signal margin (dB) = Available SNR (dB) − Required SNR (dB)
Required SNR (dB) = Eb/No + Data Rate (dBHz) − Bandwidth (dBHz)

With a 138 kbps modulation rate across a 120 kHz channel and Eb/No of 3.5 dB, the Required SNR resolves to 4.11 dB. Against the mission's target Net Signal Margin of 31.5 dB, the link must therefore deliver a minimum Available SNR of 35.61 dB.

Effective Noise Power (N₀)

Effective Noise Power (dBm) = Thermal Noise Density (kT) + Rx Bandwidth (10·log₁₀B) + Noise Figure (NF)
= −174 dBm/Hz + 50.79 dBHz + 4 dB = −119.21 dBm

Link Budget Losses

Free Space Path Loss (FSPL) was computed from the true slant range at a worst-case 25,000 ft UAV altitude over a 10 NM ground distance (slant range = 20.028 km):

FSPL(dB) = 20·log₁₀(d) + 20·log₁₀(f) + 20·log₁₀(4π/c) = 118.31 dB

EIRP & Minimum Antenna Gain

Working backward from the minimum Effective Carrier Power (−83.60 dBm) through the LCS-2 receiver chain (19 dBi antenna gain, −3 dB polarization loss, −1 dB line loss) and the total propagation loss yields a minimum required EIRP of 20.66 dBm — and, at 15 dBm transmit power with 1 dB of line/radome loss, a minimum UAV transmitter antenna gain of 7.66 dBi.

Because no real antenna reaches 100% efficiency, the required base gain was adjusted across a range of efficiency ratings. An 80%-efficient antenna was selected, driving the designed base gain up to 12.124 dBi to guarantee the 7.66 dBi realized minimum.

04   Requirements Allocation & Hardware Prediction

From Baseline Margin to Selected Hardware

An additional margin was allocated to absorb unforeseen RF losses, raising the design target from the 31.5 dB baseline to a 35.0 dB requirements allocation, and finally to a 37.40 dB predicted margin once real hardware (13.155 dBi realized antenna gain) was selected.

Hardware Selection

  • Airborne: 15.0 dBm L-Band transmitter (GMSK), 0.5 W / 80%-efficient power amplifier, 13.155 dBi omnidirectional dipole antenna, low-loss LMR-400 coax.
  • LCS-2 Ground Station: 19.0 dBi parabolic dish (18° beamwidth), 2-axis gimbal with ±45° elevation / 360° azimuth tracking, ≤4.0 dB noise figure LNA, GMSK demodulator rated for 138 kbps at ≤1×10⁻⁶ BER.

A MATLAB RF coverage analysis, run against the finalized hardware and NavPlan waypoints with rain attenuation and environmental losses included, confirmed a robust RF link throughout the operational footprint between the LCS-2 ship and the mission loiter station.

05   Conclusion

Design Verification

The UAV communication system design was validated against all derived requirements for VTOL-based ISR operations from an LCS-2 ship in the South China Sea, achieving a net signal margin of 37.40 dB against the 35.0 dB requirement. The dual-link architecture — LOS primary with BLOS backup — ensures continuous command and control across all mission phases, with BER (≤1×10⁻⁶), EIRP (26.56 dBm), and receiver noise figure (4.0 dB) all meeting or exceeding their specified thresholds.

Full requirements traceability (UAV-1 through UAV-3), communication subsystem specification (CM-1 through CM-10), and design verification status are captured in the complete design paper.
